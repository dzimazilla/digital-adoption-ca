Buying software becomes expensive when the search begins with product names and feature lists. Before comparing tools, describe the business problem in plain language: what work is going wrong, how often it happens, and what a useful improvement would look like.

“We need a CRM” is not a problem statement. “Customer information is entered in three places and often disagrees” is. The second version gives you something specific to fix and leaves open the possibility that the answer is a process change rather than new software.

## Keep the requirements short

Write down the few things the software must do for the current problem. A requirement should describe real work, such as exporting records in a usable format, supporting an essential approval, or allowing the people involved to complete the task without a workaround.

Separate those needs from features that merely look useful. Advanced automation, extensive customization, and integrations for possible future projects can make a tool more expensive and harder to operate now. Buy for the business and workflow you actually have.

A short list also makes trade-offs visible. If every feature is treated as essential, it becomes difficult to distinguish a suitable tool from a persuasive demonstration.

## Test the work, not the sales presentation

A demonstration shows what a product can do under ideal conditions. A useful trial shows whether your staff can complete the real workflow.

Use realistic, non-sensitive sample information and ask the people closest to the work to perform the common tasks. Notice where they need help, which steps still depend on another system, and whether information can be imported and exported cleanly. The important question is not whether the interface looks polished. It is whether the original problem becomes smaller without creating a new burden.

Keep the trial narrow. Testing one representative workflow properly is more useful than exploring every menu and optional feature.

## Check the full cost and the exit

The advertised licence price is only part of the cost. Consider setup, data cleanup, migration, training, support, internal staff time, and any old system that must remain active during the change.

Before committing, confirm how your information can be exported, who will administer the tool, and what happens if it does not work as expected. A reasonable exit path matters, especially when a small team cannot support a complicated migration twice.

Choose the simplest option that solves the defined problem, fits the real budget, and can be operated by the people who will use it. Extra capability is valuable only when there is a current need for it.
